# Learning from mistakes — the evidence behind the opener

The pitch claims "students learn more when they make mistakes". The precise, defensible
version: **committing to a guess before instruction improves what is learned afterwards,
even when the guess is wrong** — and errors made with confidence are corrected especially
well. Three research lines support it.

## 1. Productive failure (Kapur)

Students who attempt problems *before* being taught the method — and mostly fail — learn
the later instruction more deeply than students taught first.

- Kapur, M. (2008). Productive failure. *Cognition and Instruction*, 26(3), 379–424.
- Kapur, M. (2014). Productive failure in learning math. *Cognitive Science*, 38(5),
  1008–1022.
- Sinha, T., & Kapur, M. (2021). When problem solving followed by instruction works:
  Evidence for productive failure. *Review of Educational Research*, 91(5), 761–798.
  Meta-analysis of 53 studies; moderate positive effect overall (d ≈ 0.36), larger when the
  design is followed faithfully.

Stage line: "Research calls it productive failure — you learn a topic better after you've
guessed wrong about it."

## 2. The pretesting effect (Kornell, Bjork, Richland)

Being tested on material *before* studying it — and getting it wrong — improves later recall
compared with just studying.

- Kornell, N., Hays, M. J., & Bjork, R. A. (2009). Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance
  subsequent learning. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*,
  35(4), 989–998.
- Richland, L. E., Kornell, N., & Kao, L. S. (2009). The pretesting effect: Do unsuccessful
  retrieval attempts enhance learning? *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*, 15(3),
  243–257.

This is exactly the **prediction opener**: a question about today's topic before the topic
is taught.

## 3. The hypercorrection effect (Butterfield & Metcalfe)

Errors made with *high confidence* are more likely to be corrected after feedback than
low-confidence errors. Commitment matters.

- Butterfield, B., & Metcalfe, J. (2001). Errors committed with high confidence are
  hypercorrected. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*,
  27(6), 1491–1494.
- Metcalfe, J. (2017). Learning from errors. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 68, 465–489.
  A review of the whole field; the reference to cite if only one is cited.

This is why the opener asks for a **one-line reason**: writing the reason raises commitment,
and commitment raises the correction.

## Supporting: attention and retrieval

- Bunce, D. M., Flens, E. A., & Neiles, K. Y. (2010). How long can students pay attention
  in class? A study of student attention decline using clickers. *Journal of Chemical
  Education*, 87(12), 1438–1443. Attention lapses begin early and recur throughout a
  lecture; they are fewer during active-learning segments. Supports the **pop-ups**.
- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests
  improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255. Retrieval practice
  beats re-reading. Supports pop-ups and the review block.
- Kuznekoff, J. H., & Titsworth, S. (2013). The impact of mobile phone usage on student
  learning. *Communication Education*, 62(3), 233–252. Texting during lectures lowers
  note-taking and recall. Supports the **focus lock** — the phone must be the lesson or it
  is the enemy.

## How to use this on stage

One sentence, one name, no slide full of citations: "There's twenty years of research on
this — productive failure, Manu Kapur — guess first, learn second, and you remember more."
Keep this page for Q&A and for the education mentor.
